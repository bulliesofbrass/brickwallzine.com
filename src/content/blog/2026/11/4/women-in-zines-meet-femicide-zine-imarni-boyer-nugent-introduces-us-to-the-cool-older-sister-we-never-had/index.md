Starting off our series ‘Women in Zines,’ we’re excited to start a journey of meeting,
learning from and hearing the voices of the many women shaping zine culture today.
There have always been magazines for women, written by women, designed by women - so how do the women in this series differ?
To quote Ione Gamble in the opening chapter to “The Polyester Book of (Bad) Taste,” she writes:

“The media industry has always been less interested in helping us hone our own
interest and more intent on selling us an aspirational lifestyle only achievable through
the purchasing of products. The self hatred women’s media inspired within us is not a
quirk but rather the whole point."

Ultimately, women’s media has always centred on selling, pointing out insecurities and
forging a stereoptical, global female identity based on the products we’re meant to buy. On
the contrary, zine culture is based on individuality, independent publishing and the idea of
freedom of expression, particularly to spread news about inequalities in gender and race as
well as across the ‘establishment.
’ Women in zines are those who share their craft, their art,
and their voice to express real thoughts - unfiltered and without reservation. In this series we
will be exploring and talking with female-led zines, understanding their take on zine culture,
individuality and their message to women in this field. Brick Wall wants to celebrate a sector
of media that genuinely serves women as opposed to tearing them down.

Today we arrange a call with Imarni, creator of Femicide zine - an independent publication in
the same bold font as the riot grrrl movement. Imarni doesn’t just make zines, she charges
them with a feminist manifesto, which raises awareness about the often overlooked reality of
murder geared towards women.
Imarni began her journey in fashion design, which she honestly expresses to us ‘didn’t fill the
bill’
, but converted to zine-making in a final year project. She is currently in her second year
of a Masters degree in fine art and her online Instagram portfolio is popping!
We meet Imarni at Nottingham Zine Festival, where her stand was far from quiet. Imarni told
us it was her second year on the trot but this one felt a lot bigger - it seemed that word had
gotten out. Spread across three stories in Nottingham Central Library, the fest was packed
full of people genuinely interested in pieces of art, zines and forming a community. We all
agreed how amazing it was that it attracted so much foot traffic, especially around Femicide
Zine.

Brick Wall: What was your first encounter with zine culture and how would you say it
shaped your interest in making one?
Imarni Boyer-Nugent: I think it would have been going to the local library or arts archive
where they have samples, so I think it must have started like that when I was at college. I
think I was on a trip to an archive and saw some zines there. They weren't riot grrrl zines,
more like general fashion, but I thought “Oh my god these are so cool”
. I was already
interested in alt music and that kind of scene and I realised they’re totally connected - I just
didn’t know that yet because I was only fifteen. Yeah it must have been that and it really
made me think okay I can do whatever I want!
BW: You have a very distinct, non-mainstream zine so it makes sense. We began to
admire Femicide Zine’s audience and approach, revealing how much the Instagram
page caught our eye in its bold, not-holding-back graphics that jumped out at us.
What was it like putting the first zine together? You have done 9 in total so it’s a long
way to look back on.
