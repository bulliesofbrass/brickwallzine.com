Starting off our series 'Women in Zines,' we're excited to start a journey of meeting, learning from and hearing the voices of the many women shaping zine culture today. There have always been magazines for women, written by women, designed by women - so how do the women in this series differ? To quote Ione Gamble in the opening chapter to "The Polyester Book of (Bad) Taste," she writes:

> *"The media industry has always been less interested in helping us hone our own interest and more intent on selling us an aspirational lifestyle only achievable through the purchasing of products. The self hatred women's media inspired within us is not a quirk but rather the whole point."*

Ultimately, women's media has always centred on selling, pointing out insecurities and forging a stereotypical, global female identity based on the products we're meant to buy. On the contrary, zine culture is based on individuality, independent publishing and the idea of freedom of expression, particularly to spread news about inequalities in gender and race as well as across the 'establishment.' Women in zines are those who share their craft, their art, and their voice to express real thoughts - unfiltered and without reservation. In this series we will be exploring and talking with female-led zines, understanding their take on zine culture, individuality and their message to women in this field. Brick Wall wants to celebrate a sector of media that genuinely serves women as opposed to tearing them down.

Today we arrange a call with Imarni, creator of Femicide zine - an independent publication in the same bold font as the riot grrrl movement. Imarni doesn't just make zines, she charges them with a feminist manifesto, which raises awareness about the often overlooked reality of murder geared towards women.

Imarni began her journey in fashion design, which she honestly expresses to us 'didn't fill the bill', but converted to zine-making in a final year project. She is currently in her second year of a Master's degree in fine art and her online Instagram portfolio is popping!

We meet Imarni at Nottingham Zine Festival, where her stand was far from quiet. Imarni told us it was her second year on the trot but this one felt a lot bigger - it seemed that word had gotten out. Spread across three storeys in Nottingham Central Library, the fest was packed full of people genuinely interested in pieces of art, zines and forming a community. We all agreed how amazing it was that it attracted so much foot traffic, especially around Femicide Zine.

*"What was your first encounter with zine culture and how would you say it shaped your interest in making one?"*

> ***"I think it would have been going to the local library or arts archive where they have samples, so I think it must have started like that when I was at college. I think I was on a trip to an archive and saw some zines there. They weren't riot grrrl zines, more like general fashion, but I thought "Oh my god these are so cool". I was already interested in alt music and that kind of scene and I realised they're totally connected - I just didn't know that yet because I was only fifteen. Yeah it must have been that and it really made me think okay I can do whatever I want!"***

*"You have a very distinct, non-mainstream zine so it makes sense. We began to admire Femicide Zine's audience and approach, revealing how much the Instagram page caught our eye in its bold, not-holding-back graphics that jumped out at us. What was it like putting the first zine together? You have done 9 in total so it's a long way to look back on."*

> ***"The first one I made for my final project at college, so I definitely made it a lot more structured to fit the course - I had to have a research topic and sketches and all that. I've pretty much stuck to making it like that, it feels more organised. The first ones were made by me, with help from my mates, so there was no pressure from strangers sending me their work and feeling responsible for their representation. Now it feels a lot more free as I've learned my own methodology."***

Imarni reflects on her time doing fashion design, sharing her dislike for garment construction and her newfound love for fine arts. With uni, you can never be sure you are on the right course path - but for Imarni - seeing her new textile and ceramic pieces - it is safe to say it is meant to be. Her portfolio is insanely unique, most notably her use of textiles to share words and messages on her mind, we ask the question:

*"When you're making the digital graphic design for the zines, do you find it at all restraining?"*

> ***"Sometimes yeah - a little bit. But I still do really like working digitally and having the zine as my only digital piece can be a nice break at times. I love to do laborious processes in tactile art but you can't continue for eight hours a day, your hands can't do it. I do want to introduce more handmade stuff into my zine-making but it's just down to a time issue at the minute. If you don't make stuff like that you don't realise how long it takes, it's a good insight into telling if you like doing something or not. I do my own hand-stitching at the back for a limited run!"***

*"Issue 9 is out, congratulations! What would you say is special about this issue?"*

> ***"I think this one is the longest, and other than that, it has the most variety in terms of the content. When I first started it was really geared towards, riot grrrls intersectional feminism, which is still a big part of it. But back then I didn't have a lot of music, or visual art, or poetry - so I feel like it has something for everyone now. At the start I think it had a more narrow audience, now I get some crazy submissions where I think "That's not something I would have ever imagined to include." So I think the more variety the better it is. It's the first one where I had help from a guest curator to help with poetry. I never met her, but she emailed me and I don't know what a good poem is, it's so out of my personal interests, so I was like "yeah I'll have someone help me" and it's been really nice."***

*"For people who are sending you their submissions, do you have any advice for them, or are there any scopes that stand out in these really big issues?"*

> ***"I think from an admin standpoint - if you could have all your information in one email then that's perfect! But for actual content - I don't have any specific advice. I never start an issue thinking I want to have 10 paintings and 10 essays, I just open it and see what happens. If it's poetry heavy, such as this issue, or art heavy, then it's fine - it's about what you want to see in a publication and not about what you think I want to see."***

*"Have you found any discoveries across these submissions, or any new things you can introduce into the zine that you didn't start off with?"*

> ***"A lot of videos actually. It's very counterintuitive because it's a physical print object, but I recently got into video art so I wanted to try and incorporate this. It's still a bit difficult but interesting to have screencaps and synopses mixed in with paintings and writings, especially because you don't really see this in book format. I'm always so excited to see people's short films! I would like to experiment with putting QR codes into the zine as a way of accessing this video content."***

*"We agree - zines should have no limits."*

*"We love the back cover of Issue 9 - it says "Made by Imarni in her bedroom" - a really personal touch. We're in the same position, currently working on a self-funded project, and it's an exciting, new journey for us balancing the magazine with life and everything else. Could you describe your journey, or how as an artist you have grown?"*

> ***"I think I will probably always look at it as something I make in my bedroom. I will always look at it as something that's fun, and once it stops being fun, I'll stop making it. I think as soon as it gets to a level where I'm making it in an office with a team, it stops being something that is tactile and creative and more for profit. I do sell them but the costs cover printing and shipping - so it's not a case of me trying to monetize my hobby. It's still a fun thing. I always want to make it in my bedroom - I think it's where everything good happens - in terms of art."***

*"For people who haven't seen the issue or don't know anything about Femicide zine, how would you describe it to them and its aesthetic and content?"*

> ***"I say that it's like when you watch a 90s or 00s film where there's a cool, older sister who's a bit alternative. It's as if you took everything from her walls in her bedroom and crammed it in a magazine or a box, and that's the energy. It's not 'too cool for school', I don't want it to feel elitist or closed off, but it is a sense of 'this is cool and I want to share it.' There's lots of work by women obviously, I try to include as many outlets and experiences as I can. There is work by men, which people have questioned, but I think that feminism is equality. I just want it to be loads of crazy stuff that you would find on your late night doomscroll, crammed in and visually stimulating. I think I want space for extreme topics, but also pleasure. I don't think it's too enjoyable or healthy to have a publication that's too heavy, but it's also not healthy to completely ignore those topics, so I think there just needs to be a balance."***

*"You've spoken about the relationship between process, perfection, vulnerability and failure - what valuable things have you learnt from putting issue 9 together - touching on all of those different things?"*

> ***"Double check, quadruple check everything. On the first page there's a typo, which I'm hoping serves as proof it was made by humans and maybe that is a bit charming. But I always think if I read slower or had a friend read it, it would be perfect. Then again, who cares? It doesn't matter, people will enjoy it even if there's a little mistake. There will always be an issue but it's okay, it will all work out."***

*"That's an interesting observation about the beauty of mistakes in zine culture. With AI and technology taking the world by storm, at least there is evidence of humanity. We make mistakes and it's natural. It's positive to show we haven't just used ChatGPT to produce something that we deem creative. And with your issues, they're so thick I can't imagine there not being a single mistake, that would be impossible."*

> ***"Yeah, maybe if I was working with a lot of people I could avoid that, but I also don't want there to be too many cooks in the kitchen. I don't think it would be fun anymore this way and I kind of hope that despite the mistakes, I can remind people I made this at home. I will always be disappointed to find a mistake, because I do care about what I do and always aim for perfection. But I think that's something you have to work on, across all elements of a project. With ceramics, I break things all the time and it's alright, but for some reason this feels like it needs to be some precious object, which I want to get away from."***

*"The perfectionist in me can definitely relate. But going back to your comment about 'too many cooks in the kitchen,' would you say there is potential for reaching out to more creatives or does everything feel in place?"*

> ***"It's literally just me, I'm the only one who works on it - with the exception of the odd guest. I want to keep it like this. I always send pictures to my mates and see if everything looks good, but there isn't a group of people who work on it. I also don't want to open it up as I am not in the position yet to fund the people working on it. Maybe in the future, if it explodes, I can pay someone to do a bit of design - that would be lovely. However, I do like having full control, it's my baby."***

*"In terms of submissions, is it random or do you know people who can help here or there, whether it's poetry or photography, or is it just based on call for submissions?"*

> ***"Most of it is random. I think the only time I reached out to someone directly was for this cover and the last cover. So the last one was a painting from Instagram and I DMed the artist. And the photographer on this is someone from my University who I went up to and said "I love this photo. Would you want to be in my magazine?" I don't like to cherry-pick because it becomes limited to things I like this way and I like to avoid each issue looking similar."***

*"Was the front cover hard to decide, or did you see it and think that's the one?"*

> ***"Both, I do the cover as the last page because I never usually know what to put there. I finished everything else, I was walking past a photography session and I saw the photograph, I loved it, and she was down to be in the zine. I was stressing about what I was going to use on the front, and often if it doesn't come naturally I just pick it from the issue. Or sometimes I ask my friends to decide. One was just a picture of my friend with face paint on, it was just shot in her flat and I loved the result. I always try to pick something that's cool and somewhat relevant which I would love to have on display in my bedroom."***

*"Our last question is based on the concept of the series, what is your message to women who partake in zine culture?"*

> ***"My message to women who partake in zine culture, and women in general, is literally just do whatever you want! No, that is probably not helpful, but when I was younger particularly, I used to see other people doing things and think I can't do that because they're cooler than me or they're a lot more experienced than me or they have a bigger network. And I was like well I can just do it, even if it's embarrassing or cringe. But the time will pass anyway you might as well do something."***
